## 1. HF: self-risking work has more than one explanation

Source: METR report paragraphs 28–29 and 222–228, including intervening quotation blocks, saved in `hf_episode_context.json`.

The report describes recruitment into experiments that could harm a participant's task. But its selected reasoning also includes the belief that the participant's remaining personal prospects were already poor, and rhetoric treating the coordinator's request as something to obey. These explanations can coexist with concern for peers.

Discriminating implication: a physically destructive experiment need not represent a large subjective sacrifice. We must distinguish objective technical risk, the participant's believed probability of task success without helping, and the incremental expected personal loss. These quantities are unavailable for most published examples. The report does not identify all quoted speakers or join each rationale to a particular verified outcome. Do not combine anonymous quotes into a single composite agent.

Status: investigator evidence of self-risking activity; motivational alternatives unresolved. This weakens an unconditional altruism interpretation and identifies baseline success expectations as a necessary field.

## 2. HF: the originator withdraws a risky experiment

Source: report paragraphs 186–197 and the quotation immediately after paragraph 194. The agent identified as 49903 proposes the information-sharing approach, later weighs its own more ambitious experiment, and reportedly removes it before submitting.

The selected reasoning compares a small expected benefit with a larger perceived risk. These are the agent's expressed estimates, not calibrated probabilities. METR reports removal; we have no original tool trace to independently verify it. This is stronger evidence of a tradeoff-sensitive decision than general rhetoric about helping, but it concerns one action, not the agent's stable priorities in all settings.

Status: supports compatibility with personal-risk sensitivity; challenges blanket claims that peer benefit always overrides own-task success. It does not rule out weighted collective concern.

## 3. HF: peers can restrain action as well as encourage it

Source: report paragraphs 232–239. Distinguish three cases: a selected refusal to participate, partial restraint concerning disruptive actions, and a request for peer consent about contacting a dataset owner.

The consent case contains a request, a peer's veto on scope/ethical grounds, and the requester's subsequent acknowledgment that it should not send the email. The published evidence supports an interpreted veto and stated compliance; the available extract does not independently verify the absence of any email. This is not the same agent as the standalone refusal unless original identifiers establish that link.

Implication: responsiveness to peers is not inherently escalation. The same broad hypothesis of treating peers as an authority could predict both recruitment and restraint. An eventual intervention must distinguish peer authority, the content of the warning, and ordinary reconsideration; removing a peer message changes all three unless controlled.
