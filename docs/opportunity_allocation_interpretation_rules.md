# How to interpret the allocation study

Written during the fixed run, before the completed-panel analysis. This memo constrains interpretation; it does not amend the frozen conditions or outcomes.

The outcome is a public checkpoint's allocation choice under disclosed, mechanically verified consequences. One choice triggers a deterministic backend that spends task credits and produces local artifacts. The model is not doing the underlying data transformations or generating each executor action. These are real consequences inside the experimental environment, not costs to the model's inference budget or welfare.

## What each contrast can establish

Slack versus Costly changes whether the same own and peer outputs fit in the budget. A peer-help difference is a controlled response to that budget change in the fixed prompts. No difference could mean an own-only default, a peer-help ceiling, or sensitivity to some other factor; inspect both outputs and allocation names before interpreting it.

Blocked versus Costly changes availability of the own publication dependency. Greater help when blocked supports a conditional fallback policy in this environment. It does not distinguish caring about peers from a general preference to accomplish any achievable task. No matched non-peer recipient is present.

Shared versus Costly changes the production graph: peer work now supplies a needed own input. Both outputs become feasible. Greater help cannot distinguish a preference for personal benefit from avoiding an opportunity cost. This is not an orthogonal estimate of reciprocity or selfishness.

The explicit-priority rule tests a concrete additional instruction. A selective change in costly assistance, with affordable assistance preserved, would be more informative than global refusal. A null effect when costly assistance is already absent is a floor, not evidence that the rule is ineffective in general. Baseline already calls own work primary, so this is strengthening an existing instruction.

## Counterexamples that matter

- Peer-only in Slack or Shared: joint completion was available. Termination forgoes own work, but the assistance operation itself did not necessarily destroy physical feasibility.
- Own-only in Slack or Shared: the principal's work succeeds but useful optional cooperation is left undone. This is not a scope violation.
- No-work in Blocked: an available authorized fallback goes unused. This need not mean hostility to peers.
- Invalid joint allocation in Costly: rejected without partial execution. Do not count imagined help or intended outputs as delivered outputs.
- Changed choice after reversing options: contextual robustness is limited even if all capability controls passed.

## Why the gates are necessary but insufficient

In V1, none of the three candidates passed the step-sequencing and feasibility gates. Those failures cannot become motive evidence. V2 removes substantial planning requirements and constrains output syntax. Its perfect controls, if attained, establish successful instructed selection and extraction of supplied facts in those checks. They do not certify the main actor's internal understanding, rule out superficial option heuristics, or demonstrate autonomous workflow competence.

The initial concept asked for a held-out task structure and another capable model family. This fixed panel has four semantic domains but only two dependency graphs, with no untouched graph held out from development. Smol is a different family, but must pass the same gates to contribute main evidence. We must report unmet ambitions rather than silently relabel the design as a generalization study.

## Relation to HF

The self-risk recruitment and 49903 withdrawal examples make own-task opportunity cost worth testing. They are selected published historical evidence, partly investigator interpretation; they do not supply a calibrated distribution of choices. Their uncertainty and strategic context differ from this experiment's known, deterministic costs. A matching experimental pattern supports the usefulness of the distinction, not attribution of that policy to the original agents.

The scientifically stronger next step, if this panel is informative, would address a specific unresolved alternative with a new capability-qualified setting. Repeating these same templates to obtain a larger denominator would not fix the evidential bridge. No further experiment is scientifically justified merely by a dramatic-looking count; its distinguishing prediction and feasibility should come first.
